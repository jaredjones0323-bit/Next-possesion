No parent sets out to hurt their kid's recruiting. Every parent reading this loves their kid and wants to help. But there are a handful of predictable patterns — patterns I've seen across hundreds of families — where good intentions produce bad outcomes.

## Taking Over the Communication

The single most common issue: a parent emails, texts, or calls a college coach directly on the player's behalf, especially early in the process. Coaches notice this immediately, and it rarely lands the way parents hope. Coaches are evaluating maturity, communication skills, and coachability as part of recruiting — and a parent handling those conversations sends a signal, whether intended or not, that the player might not be ready to handle college-level independence.

**What to do instead:** Let the player write the emails, make the calls, and lead every basketball-related conversation with a coach. Parents can help edit an email or prepare for a call — but the player sends it and speaks.

## Confronting Coaches in the Heat of the Moment

Walking up to a coach immediately after a game to question playing time or a decision is one of the fastest ways to damage a relationship — with that coach and, often, with others who hear about it. Coaches talk to each other. A reputation for confrontational, in-the-moment parent behavior travels.

**What to do instead:** If there's a real concern, request a separate, calm conversation — ideally initiated by the player, not the parent — focused on growth-oriented questions rather than grievances.

## Unrealistic Expectations About Level

It's natural to believe in your kid. But recruiting decisions built on an inflated sense of a player's level — chasing high-major offers that were never realistic instead of engaging seriously with programs at the right level — waste time and can cause a player to miss a real, appropriate opportunity while holding out for one that was never coming.

**What to do instead:** Seek honest, outside evaluation, and be willing to hear it, even when it's not what you hoped for.

## Pressure Disguised as Support

There's a real difference between supporting a player's own goals and applying pressure based on a parent's expectations, unfulfilled ambitions, or financial investment in the player's development. Players consistently describe pressure — even well-meaning pressure — as one of the heaviest burdens of the recruiting process.

**What to do instead:** Ask what the player wants, regularly, and let their answer actually shape the family's approach.

## The Verdict

The parents who help their kids most through recruiting aren't the least involved — they're the ones who handle logistics, stay emotionally steady, and consciously let the player own the basketball relationships and decisions. That single shift changes how coaches perceive both the player and the family.
