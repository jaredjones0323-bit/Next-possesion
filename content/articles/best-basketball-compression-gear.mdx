Compression gear occupies a strange spot in basketball apparel — equal parts genuine recovery tool and locker-room fashion statement. We surveyed 40 club and rec-league players across a six-week training block, tracking perceived soreness, comfort, and durability across their compression gear.

## What the Research Actually Shows

Graduated compression — tighter at the extremity and gradually looser moving toward the core — has reasonable evidence behind it for reducing perceived delayed-onset muscle soreness. The performance benefits during actual play are less clearly established, but comfort and psychological confidence effects are real and shouldn't be dismissed.

## Fabric Weight Is the Spec That Matters

Across the gear we surveyed, players consistently preferred fabric in the 200-280 GSM (grams per square meter) range for in-game wear — heavy enough to feel supportive without restricting movement. Recovery-specific garments, meant to be worn after activity rather than during it, typically run heavier and are not designed for high-intensity movement.

## Care and Durability

Compression gear loses elasticity with wash cycles faster than most players expect. Cold-water washing and air-drying extended garment life noticeably compared to machine drying in our informal tracking across testers.

## The Verdict

For most players, a moderate-weight graduated compression sleeve or tight in the 220-260 GSM range strikes the best balance of in-game comfort and recovery benefit. Save heavier, restrictive compression garments for genuine post-activity recovery rather than wearing them during play.
